1. Situation

Beginning with the CDC's May 1, 2026 reporting cutoff, the United States has experienced a rapidly growing multistate outbreak of cyclosporiasis. The trigger for wide public attention was the "explosive diarrhoea" framing carried by the BBC and other outlets in early July. By that point the outbreak had spread across at least 17 states in early reporting and, within two weeks, to 34 states in CDC-confirmed tallies (the CDC health advisory cites 35; several media maps show 32). As of July 13 (published July 14) the CDC-confirmed count had reached 1,645, and with more than 5,100 additional cases under analysis the combined total approached 7,000, surpassing the roughly 4,700-case US record set in 2019. By July 16 the state-reported aggregate alone had passed 7,500, and it has continued rising since. On July 22, CDC's confirmed count broke a nine-day freeze at 1,645, jumping to 4,173 across 41 states, with more than 7,400 additional cases under analysis (combined total above 11,500); see the July 22 development below. For baseline comparison, the same point in 2025 saw 249 cases nationally.

Development, July 17: the Taco Bell cluster moves from lead to on-the-record finding, but a second vehicle emerges

FDA and CDC each published official outbreak pages on July 17 stating that epidemiologic and traceback data link a five-state cluster (1,644 cases / 94 hospitalized, illness onset May 13 to July 13) to shredded iceberg lettuce served at Taco Bell restaurants in Indiana, Kentucky, Michigan, Ohio, and West Virginia. FDA states its traceback "identified convergence on a single supplier, Taylor Farms de Mexico"; Taylor Farms itself attributes the contamination to a specific independent farm (see its statement below). FDA's consumer advisory for those five states is now published, matching the scope reported in v3 exactly. This is a materially different sourcing footing than v3 carried: it is now attributed to the agencies' own published pages rather than to press reporting citing "a person familiar with the investigation."

Taylor Farms broke its silence with a public statement on July 17: it says the FDA traceback points to a specific independent farm representing less than 1% of the US iceberg lettuce supply, that it has voluntarily withdrawn all central-Mexico iceberg lettuce indefinitely, and that it has told FDA it will initiate a recall. It states no Taylor Farms-branded salads or kits are implicated, and it stops short of admitting fault, framing the response as cooperation with the investigation rather than an acknowledgment of cause.

What this is and is not, updated. For the five-state Taco Bell cluster specifically, this now clears our bar for a finding rather than a lead: two federal agencies have published outbreak pages naming the vehicle and supplier, an advisory is live, and the named company has taken recall action rather than staying silent. We raise the attribution confidence for that cluster to High. Primary-source verification (2026-07-17): the FDA outbreak page was retrieved directly and its case counts (1,644 illnesses / 94 hospitalized / 0 deaths, last onset July 13) and its "single supplier, Taylor Farms de Mexico" wording confirmed verbatim; the Taylor Farms statement was likewise retrieved and confirmed; the CDC outbreak page corroborates the same figures and naming through the FDA page and independent secondary reporting.

What it does not resolve. North Carolina's Department of Health and Human Services states that its 307 cases (May 1 to July 14, concentrated in Wake County) are not part of the Taco Bell cluster, direct evidence of a second, separate vehicle. Suspected foods in NC include parsley, cilantro, and lettuce, unconfirmed. A five-state, single-chain cluster still does not explain a 34-state outbreak; see the mismatch discussion below, now sharpened by a concrete second-vehicle example rather than a hypothetical one.

Development, July 18: the recall publishes, and its footprint is wider than the named cluster

Taylor Farms de Mexico posted its own recall notice on July 18. Per convergent secondary reporting (CNBC, CNN, Washington Post, Consumer Reports, Marler Blog), the recalled iceberg lettuce, distributed June 29 through July 16, reached 27 states: Alabama, Arkansas, Connecticut, Florida, Georgia, Iowa, Illinois, Indiana, Kansas, Kentucky, Louisiana, Massachusetts, Maryland, Michigan, Missouri, Mississippi, North Carolina, New Hampshire, New Jersey, Ohio, Oklahoma, Pennsylvania, South Carolina, Tennessee, Texas, Virginia, and Wisconsin. The recall list includes retail product, Walmart's Marketside Bagged Iceberg Salad and Shredded Iceberg Salad among them; Walmart states it has no confirmed illnesses tied to the retail product. This is a materially larger distribution footprint than the five-state Taco Bell advisory, which covers only the restaurant channel in Indiana, Kentucky, Michigan, Ohio, and West Virginia.

The North Carolina overlap. North Carolina is on this 27-state recall distribution list, the same North Carolina whose health department maintains its 307 cases are unlinked to the Taco Bell cluster. We do not read this as disconfirming NC DHHS's statement; a recalled product's presence in a state is not evidence that any specific resident consumed it or that it caused any specific illness, and NC DHHS has not revised its position. But it is a fact worth stating plainly rather than smoothing over: the same supplier's product physically reached the one state where officials have explicitly disclaimed a link to that supplier's product, and no public source we found addresses that overlap directly. It complicates a clean reading of North Carolina as a second, unrelated vehicle, without resolving the question either way.

Separately, multiple law firms filed additional civil suits this week that, for the first time, name Taylor Fresh Foods, Inc. and Taylor Farms California, Inc. directly as defendants alongside Taco Bell, including Caruso v. Taco Bell Corporation, et al., filed in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Ohio on behalf of a Geneva, Ohio plaintiff whose stool sample tested positive for Cyclospora on July 7.

Development, July 19: FDA's own import surveillance returns a direct positive test

FDA disclosed that its targeted import surveillance program, which samples product at the border independent of any illness report, collected a sample of Taylor Farms de Mexico shredded iceberg lettuce that tested positive for Cyclospora. This is a materially different kind of evidence than everything preceding it in this briefing: the July 17 attribution rested on epidemiologic and traceback convergence, "a single supplier" identified through investigation of illness patterns, while this is a direct physical detection of the organism on the implicated commodity itself.

FDA states the positive lot was detained and is not part of Taylor Farms' currently posted 27-state recall. The company says it is working to determine whether any product from that lot reached commerce or consumers' homes. No source we found discloses the lot number, ship dates, or the states or customers that lot was destined for. This leaves a live, unanswered question: whether the 27-state recall already covers the contamination this finding points to, or whether the recall's scope needs to expand.

What this changes and does not change. It strengthens the Taylor Farms attribution for the named cluster to the strongest evidentiary footing yet, a positive laboratory result, not just convergence. It does not bear on the outbreak-wide question, North Carolina's cases remain unaddressed by this finding, and it does not resolve whether the current recall is complete. This finding is drawn from secondary reporting (Detroit News, Fox Business, Marler Blog) rather than a primary FDA page.

Correction, July 20: the import-surveillance positive test was retracted as a false positive

The July 19 development above, treated at the time as "the strongest evidentiary footing yet," did not hold up. Per convergent secondary reporting (NBC News, CNBC, Newsweek, Forbes, CBS News), FDA re-reviewed the sample on July 19 and concluded it was a false positive, stating the finding "does not represent true amplification," and reportedly apologized to Taylor Farms. As of that date, there are no confirmed positive product-sample test results anywhere in this outbreak. FDA states its epidemiological and traceback conclusion, the finding that the outbreak is linked to Taylor Farms de Mexico iceberg lettuce, is unchanged.

We are correcting this in place rather than quietly dropping the earlier claim. Under our own hedge-discipline standard, the attribution for the named cluster remains a finding, not a demoted lead, because it rests on on-the-record agency naming independent of this specific lab result. But the retraction is a real setback for the case's physical evidence, and at least one outlet (Forbes) is already framing the source as "again unknown," a characterization we think overstates FDA's own position but that is itself now part of the information environment around this outbreak. It also means our own July 19 read, that this new evidence resolved the open question about whether the 27-state recall is complete, was premature: that question reverts to unresolved on other grounds, not on the basis of a since-retracted lab result. This finding, like the one it corrects, is drawn from secondary reporting rather than a primary FDA page.

Development, July 21-22: two agency-level leads

This cycle's new findings rest on search-indexed secondary sources rather than primary CDC or FDA pages, and are flagged accordingly. Two developments stand out. First, search-indexed results now show CDC's outbreak page titled "Cyclospora Outbreak Linked to Iceberg Lettuce in 5 States" and quote it recommending against "shredded iceberg lettuce from Taylor Farms de Mexico," a change from the "Cyclosporiasis Outbreak with Unknown Source" title v8 found as of Jul 14. If accurate, this closes the interagency divergence v8 flagged as its top watch item; we label it a lead pending direct confirmation, not yet a verified finding. Second, at a press conference this week, per multiple news outlets, acting FDA Commissioner Kyle Diamantas said federal officials have investigated five distinct cyclosporiasis clusters since the start of parasite season in May, of which three are now considered over; the Taco Bell cluster, the largest, remains active. No source we found states whether North Carolina's cluster is among the three closed ones or the two still open.

Development, July 22: the national confirmed count breaks its nine-day freeze

CDC's national confirmed tally, static at 1,645 since July 13, jumped to 4,173 confirmed domestic cases across 41 states, with more than 7,400 additional cases under analysis (combined total above 11,500) and a national hospitalization figure of 308, up from 141. Per the Washington Post's reporting on the update, CDC attributes the earlier freeze to its own adjudication method: the agency counts only cases states have formally reported as lab-confirmed and assumes roughly a six-week reporting lag, while states such as Michigan count confirmed and probable cases close to real time, so state totals run higher and climb faster. This is a direct, on-the-record resolution of the question this briefing has tracked as the single most overdue item since v3; see the reconciliation in section 2 and Prediction 3's re-scoring on the briefing index.

Sourcing note. The figures above are sourced to press coverage (Washington Post, ABC News, UPI) of CDC's own release. State-level updates also continued: Michigan rose to 7,171 (Jul 22, up 9% from 6,571 the day before), and North Carolina rose to 561 (Jul 21, up from 307 on Jul 14), with hospitalizations there rising to 16.

Development, July 21 to 24: Mexico disputes the attribution on the record, and FDA discloses a second, separate outbreak

Two developments this cycle cut against this briefing's published assessments and are stated here first, per our own house rule that evidence against us gets led with rather than buried. At a news conference reported Jul 21, Mexican Health Secretary David Kershenobich said Mexico's own investigation had not found evidence that the outbreak originated at the Taylor Farms de Mexico facility FDA has traced the Taco Bell cluster to. This is a named foreign government publicly disputing the named US agencies' own traceback conclusion, a materially different kind of challenge than the retracted lab test (an internal FDA correction) or the North Carolina overlap (an unresolved distribution question). FDA and CDC have not withdrawn or revised their attribution as of this cycle, so under our hedge-discipline standard the finding for the named cluster stands, but a government-level dispute is new information we are not going to understate. Separately, and on the same day, HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. said the outbreak is "under control," a claim in tension with Michigan's continued rapid case growth (see below) and CDC's own stated expectation that cases rise through August; we note the tension rather than treat either statement as authoritative over the underlying case-count data.

Separately, FDA disclosed it is investigating a second, wholly distinct cyclosporiasis outbreak of at least 72 cases, with a product, brand, or supplier described as "not yet identified" and explicitly not linked to the Taylor Farms lettuce cluster. Coverage of exactly how many concurrent cyclospora investigations FDA has open varies by outlet and by date, from "at least four active" in one wire report to the five clusters (three closed) disclosed in v9, so we do not assert a specific running total; what is confirmed across multiple outlets is that this 72-case cluster is new and distinct from the lettuce cluster. This is materially stronger, more direct evidence for Prediction 5 (attribution will not close the whole case) than the state-count inference we relied on in v10.

Taylor Farms, meanwhile, voluntarily and temporarily shut its central-Mexico processing facility, the one that produces the 5-lb shredded-iceberg bags sold to Taco Bell, Sysco, and other large distributors, as reported Jul 23, even though no product sample from that facility has ever tested positive; the case's only positive lab result remains the July 18 to 19 false positive corrected above. We read this as a precautionary supplier action taken alongside, not because of, any new physical evidence.

One lead closes. The CDC outbreak-page retitling this briefing has carried as an unconfirmed lead since v9 is now search-confirmed: search-indexed content directly quotes a CDC page titled "Cyclospora Outbreak Linked to Iceberg Lettuce in 5 States," at a URL matching CDC's outbreak-page path, with a companion "Investigation Update" page naming Taylor Farms de Mexico and reproducing the 1,644/94/0 cluster figures verbatim. This closes our single most overdue watch item since v8, though we retain the "search-indexed" label rather than "primary-verified" this cycle.

Development, July 24-25: the named cluster itself expands to nine states, and Mexico's dispute produces a negative test

The same CDC outbreak page whose retitling closed as a lead last cycle has been retitled again: "Cyclospora Outbreak Linked to Iceberg Lettuce in 9 States," up from 5. CDC added Illinois, Kansas, Oklahoma, and Pennsylvania to the cluster already comprising Indiana, Kentucky, Michigan, Ohio, and West Virginia. The cluster-specific case count rose from 1,644 to 1,947, and hospitalizations from 94 to at least 98. This is convergent across CDC's own page title and multiple independent outlets dated Jul 24 (NBC News, The Washington Post, The Hill, Healio, NewsNation, and legal-press coverage citing the same figures and state list), a stronger evidentiary footing than the search-indexed-only confirmation of the 5-state title in v11. Kansas had been tracked in this briefing's State Involvement Map as tied to the recalled product but explicitly not a Taco Bell cluster state; that distinction no longer holds under CDC's own retitling, and the map has been updated accordingly.

Separately, Mexico's dispute moved from a spoken assertion to a specific negative test. Following Health Secretary Kershenobich's Jul 21 remarks, Mexican federal authorities report that lettuce and water samples collected at the implicated Guanajuato facility tested negative for both Cyclospora cayetanensis and fecal coliforms. Mexican officials are explicit that a negative environmental test at the plant is a data point, not proof the contamination did not occur elsewhere in the same supply chain, and FDA has not withdrawn its own traceback conclusion. We treat this as a firmer form of the dispute already carried since v11, not a standalone new finding, but it is materially stronger evidence than a statement alone.

State figures and other watch items. Michigan rose to 8,176 cases as of Jul 24 (MDHHS), up 6.6% from 7,664 the day before; hospitalizations held flat at 160. Illinois, newly relevant given its addition to the named cluster, now carries a materially fresher statewide figure than this briefing previously used: IDPH reported 408 confirmed and probable cases as of Jul 21 (215 domestic, 145 travel-associated, 48 unknown, 31 hospitalized), replacing the stale, secondary-sourced 87 figure from Jul 11. No fresher figures were found this cycle for Ohio's statewide total (still 1,316, Jul 17), North Carolina (still 561, Jul 20), or CDC's national confirmed count, which has now held at 4,173/41 states across two consecutive cycles (Jul 22 to Jul 24) since the Jul 22 catch-up. The separate, 72-case FDA-disclosed outbreak is unchanged this cycle: no product or supplier identified.

Cyclosporiasis is caused by Cyclospora cayetanensis, a coccidian protozoan parasite for which humans are the only known host. Infection follows ingestion of the parasite's environmentally matured oocysts on contaminated fresh produce or in water. Crucially, all confirmed 2026 domestic cases involved no international travel in the preceding two weeks, pointing to a domestic food or water source rather than travel-acquired infection.

Clinical picture

Watery, sometimes explosive diarrhea, loss of appetite, weight loss, cramping, bloating, nausea, fatigue, and low-grade fever, in a relapsing-remitting course. Incubation averages about one week (range roughly 2 days to 2+ weeks). Untreated illness in healthy adults can persist for weeks; it is more severe and prolonged in immunocompromised people. First-line treatment is trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (Bactrim).

3. The Pathogen and Why It Is Hard to Trace

Cyclospora's biology is almost purpose-built to frustrate outbreak investigators. Oocysts are passed in stool in an unsporulated, non-infective state and require days to weeks in the environment (roughly 22-32 °C) to mature before they can infect anyone. This single fact drives two consequences: there is no person-to-person transmission, and every case traces back to an environmental or produce exposure rather than a human contact chain.

Three structural factors make source attribution slow and often inconclusive:

  • Long, fuzzy incubation. A one-week-plus lag with a wide range means patients must recall what they ate up to two weeks earlier, degrading exposure histories.
  • Non-routine laboratory detection. Cyclospora is missed by standard stool panels and needs specific testing (modified acid-fast stain, UV fluorescence microscopy, or a molecular GI-PCR panel that includes it). Many labs do not run it, causing under-ascertainment.
  • Perishable, commingled evidence. Implicated produce is usually eaten or discarded before it can be tested, and bagged-salad processing blends many source lots, turning a point-source contamination into a diffuse multistate signature.

The full mechanics of the supply-chain vector, and a historical base rate of which foods have been implicated, are in the Supply-Chain Threat Analysis.

Assessment: correlation strong, causation contested

We rate the capacity strain and correlation as High confidence: the surveillance reductions are well-documented and public-health experts have voiced concern that they slow patient interviews, trace-back, and trend analysis. We rate direct causal attribution ("the cuts caused this outbreak or its unsolved source") as Medium confidence and contested. Cyclosporiasis remains nationally notifiable through NNDSS, so case detection persists; the documented erosion is in speed, trace-back, and analysis, not in whether cases are seen at all.

The honest framing: the system can still count cases but is slower to act on them, and the food-safety trace-back apparatus is visibly stressed. This outbreak functions as a natural stress test signaling reduced readiness, whether or not the cuts are the proximate cause of any single unsolved lead.
