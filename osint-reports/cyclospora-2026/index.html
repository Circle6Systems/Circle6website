2026 US Cyclosporiasis Outbreak

Multistate · Reporting period from May 1, 2026 · Briefing v8, July 22, 2026 (data as of Jul 21)

Open-source threat-intelligence briefing on the largest domestic Cyclospora cayetanensis event on record. A parasite spread through the fresh-produce supply chain has produced 1,645 CDC-confirmed domestic cases across 34 states, with more than 5,100 under analysis and state-reported totals above 7,500, far exceeding the ~4,700-case US record set in 2019. FDA and CDC officially link a five-state Taco Bell cluster (1,644 cases / 94 hospitalized) to iceberg lettuce sourced from Taylor Farms de Mexico. A lettuce sample from FDA's own import surveillance briefly tested positive for Cyclospora, which would have been the strongest evidence in the case to date, but FDA retracted the result the next day as a false positive; the agency's epidemiological and traceback conclusion stands, but the attribution again rests on that convergence rather than a confirmed contaminated sample. Taylor Farms' recall spans 27 states, including retail bagged lettuce sold at Walmart, far broader than the five-state cluster FDA's advisory covers. North Carolina sits on that 27-state distribution list even though its health department maintains its own cases are a separate, unlinked source, a tension this briefing has not resolved. CDC's national confirmed total is still being reported unchanged since Jul 13. We assess the outbreak as a real-world stress test of degraded US biosurveillance and food-safety trace-back capacity.

Bottom Line Up Front

This is a clinically mild but epidemiologically significant outbreak. Severity is low: the illness is self-limiting, treatable with antibiotics, and not spread person-to-person. The attribution to Taylor Farms de Mexico briefly gained a materially different kind of evidence this cycle: FDA disclosed that its own targeted import surveillance had returned a direct positive laboratory test for Cyclospora on a Taylor Farms lettuce sample, not just epidemiologic convergence on a supplier. One day later, FDA reversed itself, calling the result a false positive after re-review and reportedly apologizing to Taylor Farms. FDA states its epidemiological and traceback conclusion is unchanged, so the attribution for the named cluster still clears our bar for a finding, but the case's one piece of direct physical evidence has now been withdrawn, and we say so plainly rather than let the earlier, stronger framing stand uncorrected. Taylor Farms' recall notice still covers 27 states and retail bagged product, not just the five-state restaurant cluster. That is not the same claim as "the outbreak is solved." North Carolina's health department still says its own cases are not linked to the Taco Bell cluster, yet North Carolina is itself on the 27-state recall distribution list, an overlap we flag rather than resolve. Multiple law firms have now filed suits naming Taylor Farms directly, not just Taco Bell. CDC's national confirmed count is still being reported at 1,645, apparently unmoved since Jul 13. Together these continue to point toward our original thesis: the July 2025 reduction of CDC's FoodNet active surveillance from eight pathogens to two (Cyclospora among those dropped), plus an approximately 25% workforce cut, have left a system that can still count cases and can still run a traceback, but is visibly uneven in what it discloses and when, and whose own lab results are not yet fully reliable.

What changed since v5 (Jul 18 → Jul 19). FDA disclosed that its own targeted import surveillance collected a sample of Taylor Farms de Mexico shredded iceberg lettuce at the border that tested positive for Cyclospora, a direct laboratory finding rather than an epidemiologic traceback inference. FDA states the positive lot was detained and is not part of Taylor Farms' currently posted 27-state recall; the company says it is working to determine whether any of that lot reached commerce or consumers' homes. No source we found discloses the lot number, ship dates, or which states or customers that lot was destined for, a gap Marler Blog's coverage criticized directly. This is the strongest form of evidence for the Taylor Farms attribution to date, but it also raises a live, unanswered question: whether the current 27-state recall fully captures the contaminated product, or whether this positive lot represents a gap in it.

Still unresolved from v5. Taylor Farms' own recall notice, distributed Jun 29 through Jul 16 to 27 states (AL, AR, CT, FL, GA, IA, IL, IN, KS, KY, LA, MA, MD, MI, MO, MS, NC, NH, NJ, OH, OK, PA, SC, TN, TX, VA, WI), including retail bagged product (Walmart's Marketside Bagged Iceberg Salad and Shredded Iceberg Salad), still stands as previously reported. North Carolina, whose 307 cases NC DHHS maintains are unlinked to the Taco Bell cluster, remains on that 27-state distribution list; we found no source this cycle that addresses the overlap. Litigation volume (multiple suits now naming Taylor Fresh Foods, Inc. and Taylor Farms California, Inc. directly) is unchanged from v5. CDC's national confirmed count is still being cited at 1,645 by every secondary source we found, consistent with a continuing freeze, but we still could not independently verify it against CDC's own page this cycle.

Access note, corrected in v8. v5 through v7 each carried a note stating that our outbound network access was "blocked at the proxy/gateway level for essentially every external host." That was our own diagnostic error, and we are correcting it here rather than quietly dropping it. Three unrelated failures had been generalized into one imagined blanket block: cdc.gov was returning HTTP 403 to automated bot fingerprinting and returns 200 when sent a complete browser header set; several fda.gov failures were HTTP 404s from URLs the agency had retired, which is a dead link and not a block; and taylorfarms.com was never blocked at any point. The practical consequence was that we described three cycles of findings as resting on secondary sources when primary verification was in fact available to us. Those findings have now been verified directly against CDC and FDA primary pages and they hold; see the v8 note below for the results.
Correction, v7 (Jul 19 → Jul 20): the v6 "direct positive lab test" was retracted the next day as a false positive. v6 reported that FDA's targeted import surveillance had returned a direct positive laboratory test for Cyclospora on Taylor Farms de Mexico lettuce, and called it "the strongest form of evidence for the Taylor Farms attribution to date." On July 19, per convergent secondary reporting (NBC News, CNBC, Newsweek, Forbes, CBS News), FDA re-reviewed that same sample and concluded it was a false positive, stating the finding "does not represent true amplification," and reportedly apologized to Taylor Farms. As of that date, there are no confirmed positive product-sample test results anywhere in this outbreak. FDA states its epidemiological and traceback conclusion, that the outbreak is linked to Taylor Farms de Mexico iceberg lettuce, is unchanged, so under our own hedge-discipline standard this remains a finding rather than a demoted lead. But v6's specific claim that this was now the strongest evidence in the case no longer holds, and we are correcting it here rather than quietly dropping it. We are reverting Prediction 1's v6 confidence bump (97% back to 95%), since it was based specifically on this now-retracted lab result, and revising Prediction 4 to reflect that the "capability-positive" reading v6 gave this detection no longer applies either, since the detection itself was erroneous. Everything else carried forward from v6 (the 27-state recall, the North Carolina/recall overlap, litigation naming Taylor Farms entities directly) is unaffected by this correction and stands as previously reported; we found no source this cycle that changes any of it. CDC's national confirmed count is still cited at 1,645 by the sources we found, but this remains unverified against CDC's own page for a third consecutive cycle.
What changed in v8 (Jul 20): a primary-source verification pass, and one correction against ourselves. This cycle added no new outbreak facts. It re-established direct retrieval from CDC and FDA and used it to check what v5 through v7 had published on secondary sourcing. Three results. First, our own access note was wrong, see the correction above; that is the most significant change in this version and it is a defect in our method rather than in the reporting. Second, every substantive v5 through v7 claim held. The 27-state distribution list matches FDA's recall notice exactly, the recall dates are confirmed as Jul 17 company announcement and Jul 18 FDA publication, the false-positive retraction is confirmed in FDA's own words, and CDC's national count is confirmed frozen at the Jul 13 figures on a page last updated Jul 15. Third, the Taylor Farms attribution is now on firmer ground than we credited. We had continued to hedge it as resting on epidemiologic inference plus press reporting; FDA's investigation page states plainly, "Do not eat recalled iceberg lettuce from Taylor Farms de Mexico," which is a named agency saying it on the record and clears our own standard for a finding.

New this cycle: the two agencies do not currently agree. FDA names Taylor Farms de Mexico and titles its investigation a 5-state outbreak tied to iceberg lettuce. CDC's outbreak page, dated Jul 14, is still titled "Cyclosporiasis Outbreak with Unknown Source." We are not reading this as a dispute; the likelier explanation is that CDC has not refreshed a page FDA has, which is consistent with the same publication unevenness we have tracked since v3. But readers consulting CDC directly will find no attribution at all, and that gap is worth stating plainly rather than smoothing over. We also note that FDA removed the retracted sample information from its own Jul 18 update; we have retained ours with the correction attached, which we think is the better practice for a document that asks to be trusted over time.

Sourcing upgrade. Our "cases under analysis" figure of more than 5,100 is now sourced to CDC directly, which states it "is aware of more than 5,100 cases that require further analysis to confirm the illness as domestic," rather than inferred from state aggregation. Prediction 1 is raised from 95% to 97% on FDA's on-the-record naming. This is the same 97% v6 asserted and v7 reverted, but it now rests on an agency statement rather than on a lab result that did not survive the week; we note that explicitly so the number is not mistaken for a quiet restoration.

Predictive Assessments

Predictions are time-stamped at publication and tracked for calibration. Resolution status is updated as confirming or disconfirming evidence emerges, including when the evidence cuts against us. Baseline date: July 13, 2026 (v1); updated July 15, 2026 (v2), July 16, 2026 (v3), July 18, 2026 (v4), July 19, 2026 (v5), July 20, 2026 (v6), July 21, 2026 (v7), and July 22, 2026 (v8).
Prediction 1: A leafy-green or fresh-herb vehicle

If a food vehicle is ever identified, it will be a leafy-green/salad blend or a fresh herb (basil/cilantro), consistent with every resolved US Cyclospora outbreak since 2013, not an exotic new commodity.

v2 update (Jul 15): Trending toward confirmation. Michigan officials now cite lettuce or salad greens as the probable vehicle and FDA is investigating produce, consistent with this prediction, though no vehicle is officially confirmed. Confidence raised 70% to 80%.

v3 update (Jul 16): Effectively confirmed pending official word. The reported traceback lands on iceberg lettuce, squarely inside the predicted commodity class, and on a Mexican-grown import, matching the second half of the base rate. Confidence raised 80% to 90%. We withhold full resolution only because no agency has confirmed on the record.

v4 update (Jul 17): Confirmed. FDA and CDC now officially link the five-state Taco Bell cluster to iceberg lettuce. North Carolina's suspected (unconfirmed) foods for its separate cluster, parsley, cilantro, and lettuce, also sit inside this predicted commodity class. Confidence raised 90% to 95%.

v5 update (Jul 18): No new information this cycle bears on the commodity class itself. Taylor Farms' newly published recall covers the same commodity (iceberg lettuce), reinforcing rather than changing this call. Holding at 95%.

v6 update (Jul 19): FDA's own import surveillance returned a direct positive laboratory test for Cyclospora on Taylor Farms iceberg lettuce, the strongest form of confirmation for this commodity class to date, a physical detection rather than an epidemiologic or traceback inference. Raising confidence modestly, 95% to 97%.

v7 correction (Jul 20): reverting the v6 bump. The lab result v6 cited was retracted by FDA the next day as a false positive; see the correction note above the Situation Snapshot. Since the 97% figure was based specifically on that now-withdrawn physical detection, we are reverting to the pre-v6 figure rather than keeping a confidence level whose stated basis no longer holds. This does not disconfirm the commodity-class call itself, which still rests on the epidemiologic and traceback attribution for the named cluster. Confidence back to 95%.

v8 update (Jul 21): raising to 97%, on a different basis than v6 used. FDA's outbreak investigation page, retrieved directly this cycle, instructs consumers plainly: "Do not eat recalled iceberg lettuce from Taylor Farms de Mexico." That is a named agency naming the commodity and the supplier on the record, which is the standard we set for a finding. We are returning to 97%, the same figure v6 asserted and v7 reverted, and we flag that explicitly: the number is the same but its basis is not. v6 rested it on a laboratory detection that did not survive the week; v8 rests it on an agency statement that is still standing. Confidence 95% to 97%.

Prediction 2: Source likely never conclusively named

Given the long incubation window, produce commingling, perishable evidence, and stressed trace-back capacity, there is a better-than-even chance this outbreak closes without a single conclusively identified food source.

v3 update (Jul 16): trending against us. FDA's traceback reportedly reached a named supplier, commodity, and country of origin inside roughly ten weeks. That is a materially better outcome than we forecast, and we are marking the confidence down 60% to 40%. Two reasons we are not abandoning the call outright: the attribution is not yet on the record, and a five-state restaurant cluster may account for only part of a 34-state outbreak, leaving the remainder unattributed even if Taylor Farms is formally named. The 2013 precedent is instructive here: FDA named Taylor Farms de Mexico and still concluded that not all cases were related to each other.

v4 update (Jul 17): disconfirmed for the named cluster, open for the outbreak as a whole. FDA and CDC named a supplier, commodity, and country of origin on the record within about eleven weeks, for the Taco Bell cluster that call is now clearly wrong. We are splitting the resolution: disconfirmed for that cluster, but North Carolina's separate, still-unattributed cases show a real share of the 34-state case load remains without a named source, exactly the mechanism this prediction anticipated. Lowering overall confidence 40% to 25%, reflecting that the prediction's narrow form failed while its broader spirit is holding for part of the outbreak.

v5 update (Jul 18): No change to the named cluster's resolution. Holding at 25%.

v6 update (Jul 19): The import-surveillance positive test strengthens the named cluster's attribution further but does not bear on the outbreak-wide question this prediction is now tracking. Holding at 25%.

v7 update (Jul 20): The v6 lab result this prediction referenced was retracted (see the correction note above), but this prediction's split resolution never depended on that specific test, only on the on-the-record agency naming for the cluster and North Carolina's unresolved status for the outbreak-wide half. No change to either. Holding at 25%.

Prediction 3: Confirmed count converges upward toward state tallies

As the under-analysis pool is adjudicated, the CDC-confirmed figure will rise substantially above the v1 figure of 843, narrowing (but not closing) the gap to the state aggregate, validating that the true burden sits well above the confirmed count.

v2 update (Jul 15): Confirmed. CDC-confirmed cases roughly doubled to 1,645 in two days and the under-analysis pool grew to >5,100 (nearly 7,000 combined), exceeding the ~4,700-case 2019 US record.

v3 correction (Jul 16): we called this too early. The prediction had two halves. The count did rise sharply (843 to 1,645), which v2 marked as confirmation. But the second half, narrowing the gap, has not happened and has in fact reversed: CDC has been static at 1,645 since Jul 13 while the state aggregate climbed past 7,500, so the gap widened from roughly 2.4x to 4.6x. Marking this back down from Confirmed to Partially confirmed and reducing confidence 80% to 55%. The underlying claim (true burden far exceeds the confirmed count) is holding; our model of how the numbers would reconcile was wrong. Adjudication appears to be a batch process with multi-day latency, not the continuous convergence we assumed.

v4 update (Jul 17): the gap kept widening, and our own charitable explanation looks weaker. CDC's national count is still static at 1,645, a five-day freeze, while Michigan alone is now above 5,000 and the state aggregate sits well past 7,500. More importantly, agencies published a fresh, current, cluster-specific count (1,644/94) in the same period they left the national topline untouched, which undercuts the pure "batch latency" reading we gave this in v3. We are lowering confidence further, 55% to 40%, and flagging this as the item most deserving of harder scrutiny next cycle.

v5 update (Jul 18): Every secondary source we found this cycle still cites 1,645, consistent with a continued (now apparent sixth-day) freeze, but we could not independently confirm this against CDC's own page this cycle due to a network access limitation (see the "What changed" note above), so we cannot rule out that sources are simply repeating a stale figure rather than confirming no change. Lowering confidence slightly, 40% to 35%, to reflect that verification gap rather than new disconfirming evidence. This remains the item most deserving of a direct primary-source check.

v6 update (Jul 19): Same verification gap persists; every secondary source we found still cites 1,645, and we still could not fetch CDC's own page directly. No new disconfirming or confirming evidence this cycle. Holding at 35%.

v7 update (Jul 20): Same verification gap, now a third consecutive cycle. Every source we found still cites 1,645; CDC's own page remained unreachable. Holding at 35% rather than lowering further on unverified secondary citations alone.

Prediction 4: Surveillance-capacity becomes the durable story

Independent of the clinical outcome, the lasting policy narrative will be the FoodNet reduction and CDC workforce cuts as a case study in eroded outbreak-response readiness, cited in future foodborne-illness and public-health-funding debates.

v3 update (Jul 16): Strengthened. Two developments feed this narrative directly: CDC's confirmed count stalling for three days while state totals surged, and the supplier lead reaching the public through a leaked source to reporters rather than an FDA advisory. Whatever the eventual attribution, "the press named the supplier before the agency did" is a durable talking point. Confidence raised 75% to 80%.

v4 update (Jul 17): Reinforced further. The advisory and recall arrived only after five days of a frozen national count and mounting press pressure, and a fresh cluster-specific figure landed before the topline did. Confidence raised 80% to 83%.

v5 update (Jul 18): No material new evidence this cycle bears directly on this prediction. Holding at 83%.

v6 update (Jul 19): mixed evidence, not simply reinforcing. FDA's targeted import surveillance catching a contaminated lot at the border is a capability-positive data point, proactive federal testing worked, which cuts against a pure "eroded surveillance" reading and is worth stating plainly rather than folding only the negative half of this story into the narrative. But the same disclosure did not include the lot number, ship dates, or destination states, a transparency gap Marler Blog's coverage criticized directly, and that gap continues to feed the durable-story thesis on the disclosure side even as the detection side cuts the other way. Net effect: lowering confidence slightly, 83% to 81%, to reflect that the evidence this cycle is genuinely two-directional rather than one-sided.

v7 correction (Jul 20): the "capability positive" half of the v6 read does not survive the retraction. The catch that v6 credited as proactive federal testing working was itself a false positive, meaning the capability point cuts the other way: rather than "surveillance caught contamination," the accurate description is "surveillance produced an erroneous result that required a public correction and apology." That is a capacity-strain data point, not a capacity-strength one. Combined with the pre-existing disclosure-gap criticism (still unresolved) and a still-unverified national topline entering a third cycle, we are not just reverting to 83%, we are raising above it: confidence up 81% to 85%.

Prediction 5: Attribution will not close the case (new in v3)

Even if FDA formally names Taylor Farms iceberg lettuce, a substantial share of the 34-state case load will remain unattributed to that vehicle. The reported cluster spans five states and one restaurant chain; the outbreak does not. We expect the final picture to include either much broader distribution of the same supplier's product beyond Taco Bell, or a second concurrent vehicle. The 2013 analogue closed exactly this way, with FDA noting that not all cases were directly related.

v4 update (Jul 17): trending confirmed. North Carolina's health department states its 307 cases are not part of the Taco Bell cluster, direct evidence of exactly the second-vehicle mechanism this prediction anticipated, one week after we wrote it. FDA's own attribution remains scoped to five states. Confidence raised 70% to 85%.

v5 update (Jul 18): complicated, not simply confirmed further. Taylor Farms' newly published recall distributes the same implicated product to 27 states, including North Carolina, the very state whose cases we cited as evidence of a clean second vehicle. That does not disconfirm NC DHHS's statement that its cases are unlinked, but it means the "broader distribution of the same supplier's product" half of this prediction and the "second concurrent vehicle" half are no longer cleanly separable in North Carolina's case: the same supplier's product reached NC through non-Taco-Bell channels, so a future finding that NC's cases trace to Taylor Farms after all would resolve this prediction's first clause rather than its second. We are lowering confidence slightly, 85% to 78%, to reflect this added ambiguity rather than raising it further on an unexamined assumption.

v6 update (Jul 19): The import-surveillance positive test bears on attribution confidence for the named cluster, not on North Carolina's unresolved status. No source we found this cycle addresses the NC overlap. Holding at 78%.

v7 update (Jul 20): The retracted lab result (see correction note above) does not bear on the North Carolina overlap either; no source this cycle addresses it. Holding at 78%.
