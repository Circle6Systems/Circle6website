Executive Overview

2026 US Cyclosporiasis Outbreak - Leadership Brief
Product Executive Overview
Audience Leadership / Non-technical
TLP CLEAR
Published August 18, 2026 (v29, federal data as of Aug 17)
Read time ~2 minutes
Confidence High (facts) / High, disputed by Mexico (cluster attribution) / Medium (outbreak-wide attribution)
The one thing to know

A produce-borne parasite has caused the largest domestic Cyclospora outbreak on record, now well past the ~4,700-case 2019 US high. The illness itself is mild, treatable, and not contagious between people, so direct health risk to any single organization is low. The Taco Bell / Taylor Farms de Mexico iceberg-lettuce cluster has grown to 17 states, adding Maine and Massachusetts since Aug 5 (on top of Arkansas, Iowa, Missouri, Nebraska, New Hampshire and North Carolina added earlier), now 9,481 confirmed cases, 398 hospitalizations and 2 deaths, per CDC's outbreak page updated Aug 13. It is the lettuce attribution that Mexico's own Health Secretary has publicly disputed on the record, saying his country's investigation found no evidence the outbreak originated there; Mexican authorities separately report a negative Cyclospora test on lettuce and water samples from the implicated Guanajuato facility. FDA and CDC have not withdrawn their conclusion. Taylor Farms has voluntarily, temporarily closed the implicated Mexico processing facility even though no product sample from that facility has ever tested positive; the case's only lab-positive result remains the retraction described below. FDA also discloses two wholly separate ongoing outbreaks, the larger at 93 cases, with products still not identified. Taylor Farms' recall itself reaches beyond the named cluster: at least 31 states, including retail bagged lettuce sold at Walmart. What belongs on your radar now: the cluster expansion put North Carolina inside the federal count, but NCDHHS's own page (updated Aug 18, 929 cases / 32 hospitalizations) still says its cases do not appear associated with the Taco Bell outbreak and points to parsley and cilantro, an open federal-vs-state disagreement we carry rather than resolve. CDC's national confirmed count rose this cycle, its second consecutive weekly rise, to 15,716 cases across 47 states, the District of Columbia, and Puerto Rico (up from 13,895; data through Aug 17, posted Aug 18; CDC updates it weekly, and it resumed publishing national hospitalization and death totals this cycle: 828 hospitalizations and 2 deaths). All of it points to the same backdrop of recently reduced federal food-safety surveillance, a lab result that had to be publicly walked back, and a foreign government publicly contesting the US agencies' own conclusion with an evidentiary result of its own.

What Happened

Since May 1, 2026, the US has seen a surge of cyclosporiasis, an intestinal illness caused by the microscopic parasite Cyclospora cayetanensis. It spreads when people eat fresh produce or drink water contaminated with the parasite. It does not spread person-to-person. Symptoms are watery, sometimes "explosive" diarrhea, cramps, nausea, and fatigue, which can relapse if untreated but respond to a common antibiotic (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole).

As of July 13 (published July 14), the CDC had confirmed 1,645 domestic cases across 34 states (the CDC health advisory cites 35; several media maps show 32), with 141 hospitalizations and zero deaths. That figure then sat frozen for nine days before jumping to 4,173 cases across 41 states on July 22, with hospitalizations rising to 308 (see the "New this cycle" note below). Two larger numbers circulate alongside the confirmed figure: more than 7,400 cases are still under analysis, and Michigan alone reported 7,171 cases as of July 22, against a normal yearly baseline near 50. These are different measurements, not a contradiction, and we explain the distinction in the full briefing. For scale: the same point last year saw 249 cases nationally.

On July 17, the picture resolved for part of the outbreak. FDA and CDC published official outbreak pages linking a five-state cluster (1,644 cases / 94 hospitalized, Indiana, Kentucky, Michigan, Ohio, West Virginia) to shredded iceberg lettuce sourced from Taylor Farms de Mexico, and FDA's consumer advisory for those five states was published. Taylor Farms broke its silence with a public statement and said it would initiate a recall. It is also not the whole story: North Carolina's health department says its own cases are not linked to the Taco Bell cluster, direct evidence of a second, separate vehicle.

On July 18, Taylor Farms posted the recall itself, and its scope is bigger than the five-state cluster: the recalled product, distributed June 29 through July 16, reached 27 states, including retail bagged lettuce sold at Walmart under its Marketside brand. Walmart reports no confirmed illnesses tied to the retail product. North Carolina, the state whose own health department says its cases are unlinked to the Taco Bell cluster, is on that 27-state list, a fact we surface rather than resolve. Separately, multiple law firms filed suits this week that for the first time name Taylor Farms' operating entities directly as defendants, not just Taco Bell.

Then, FDA disclosed that its own targeted import surveillance, sampling at the border rather than investigating a reported illness, collected Taylor Farms de Mexico shredded iceberg lettuce that tested positive for Cyclospora on July 18, which would have been the strongest single piece of evidence for the attribution to date, a direct laboratory finding rather than a statistical traceback inference. On July 19, FDA reversed itself: it re-reviewed the sample and concluded the result was a false positive ("does not represent true amplification"), and reportedly apologized to Taylor Farms. FDA states its epidemiological and traceback conclusion, that the outbreak is linked to Taylor Farms de Mexico iceberg lettuce, is unchanged. As of July 19, there is no confirmed positive product-sample test result anywhere in this outbreak.

New this cycle (v9). The figures below are sourced to search-indexed secondary reporting rather than primary pages, and are flagged accordingly. Michigan's case count has continued climbing sharply, from 5,002 (Jul 17) to 6,571 as of Jul 21 per MDHHS. North Carolina's reported case count has risen from 307 to more than 500 over the same window; NC DHHS still maintains those cases are unlinked to the Taco Bell cluster. Search-indexed results also show CDC's own outbreak page apparently renamed to explicitly name Taylor Farms de Mexico iceberg lettuce, closing the gap we previously noted between FDA's and CDC's public framing, a lead pending direct confirmation. Separately, at a press conference this week, acting FDA Commissioner Kyle Diamantas said federal officials have investigated five distinct cyclosporiasis clusters since May, three of which are now considered over; whether North Carolina's cluster is among them is not yet known.

New this cycle (v10): the national confirmed count broke its freeze. On Jul 22, CDC's confirmed count rose from 1,645 to 4,173 across 41 states, with hospitalizations rising from 141 to 308 and more than 7,400 additional cases under analysis. Per the Washington Post, CDC attributes the earlier gap to its own reporting method (a roughly six-week confirmation lag versus states' faster, broader counts), not concealment, and this update is consistent with that explanation. Michigan rose further to 7,171 (up 9% in a day) and North Carolina to 561. These figures are sourced to press coverage of CDC's own release.

At a Jul 21 news conference, Mexican Health Secretary David Kershenobich said Mexico's own investigation found no evidence the outbreak originated at the Taylor Farms de Mexico facility FDA has traced the Taco Bell cluster to. FDA and CDC have not withdrawn their traceback conclusion. Separately, HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. said the outbreak is "under control" the same day, a claim in tension with Michigan's continued case growth. FDA also discloses two distinct ongoing outbreaks, the larger at 93 cases, with products "not yet identified," explicitly unlinked to the lettuce cluster. Taylor Farms voluntarily, temporarily closed its central-Mexico processing facility as US cases kept climbing, even though no sample from that facility has ever tested positive. Michigan rose further to approximately 7,644 to 7,664 cases with hospitalizations up sharply to 160. CDC's national confirmed count has not moved from 4,173/41 states in any source found this cycle. Separately, the CDC outbreak-page retitling flagged as an unconfirmed lead since v9 is now search-confirmed, closing that watch item.

New this cycle (v12): the named cluster itself expanded to nine states, and Mexico's dispute produced a negative test. CDC retitled its outbreak page again, from "5 States" to "9 States," adding Illinois, Kansas, Oklahoma, and Pennsylvania to the cluster. The cluster-specific count rose from 1,644 to 1,947 cases and hospitalizations from 94 to at least 98, confirmed convergently across CDC's own page title and multiple independent outlets dated Jul 24. Separately, Mexican federal authorities report that lettuce and water samples collected at the implicated Guanajuato facility tested negative for Cyclospora and fecal coliforms, a firmer form of Mexico's dispute than Secretary Kershenobich's Jul 21 statement alone, though officials caution a negative plant test does not by itself resolve where in the supply chain contamination occurred, and FDA has not withdrawn its conclusion. Michigan rose further to 8,176 cases (up 6.6% in a day) with hospitalizations flat at 160.

FDA's own active investigation table lists two ongoing unidentified-product Cyclospora outbreaks, alongside two further investigations whose outbreaks have ended (corrected in v19 from the four this briefing reported since v14), not the two we had been describing: the lettuce cluster, the 72-case outbreak tracked since v11, plus an 18-case and a 10-case outbreak, the latter three all with product "not yet identified." Why It Matters

Low direct severity, but no longer zero deaths

Self-limiting, treatable, and non-contagious for most. On Aug 3 Michigan MDHHS reported the outbreak's first 2 deaths (both decedents had significant underlying conditions; both ill before the Jul 17 recall), so this is no longer a fatality-free event, though MDHHS maintains cyclosporiasis is generally not life-threatening. For most organizations the operational risk remains minimal and manageable with routine food-safety hygiene.

A published recall, but not a closed outbreak, and now a disputed attribution

FDA and CDC officially link Taylor Farms de Mexico iceberg lettuce to a Taco Bell cluster now spanning 17 states, adding Maine and Massachusetts on CDC's Aug 13 outbreak page (9,481 cases / 398 hospitalized / 2 deaths), and Taylor Farms' own recall footprint is now restated at at least 31 states, including retail product. A lettuce sample that briefly tested positive for Cyclospora was retracted by FDA the next day as a false positive, and Mexico's own Health Secretary has publicly disputed the attribution on the record, saying his country's investigation found no evidence the outbreak originated there; Mexican authorities separately report a negative Cyclospora test on samples from the implicated Guanajuato facility. FDA and CDC have not withdrawn their conclusion. North Carolina is now federally counted in the cluster, yet NCDHHS's own page (Aug 18) still calls its cases a separate, unlinked source pointing to parsley and cilantro, an open federal-vs-state disagreement that is not yet resolved. FDA has also disclosed a wholly separate outbreak of 93 cases with an unidentified product. Even a 17-state restaurant/lettuce cluster and a 31-state recall footprint still do not by themselves explain a 47-state confirmed national footprint. Buyers now have an authoritative "avoid product X" for the named cluster; they still do not have one for the outbreak as a whole.

Capacity signal

In July 2025 the CDC's FoodNet surveillance was cut from eight pathogens to two, dropping Cyclospora, amid an approximately 25% workforce reduction. The outbreak remains a live stress test of that reduced capacity. CDC's confirmed count breaking a nine-day freeze with a large, coherent catch-up update was a capability-positive signal, but this cycle adds a foreign government publicly disputing the lead US attribution, a Cabinet-level "under control" statement in tension with continued case growth, and a second, unexplained outbreak surfacing concurrently, all layered onto an otherwise strained system.

Bottom Line

For the public and most employers: practical, not alarming. Wash produce, know the symptoms, and see a clinician (and ask specifically for Cyclospora testing) for prolonged diarrhea. FDA's advisory now spans a 17-state cluster (Arkansas, Iowa, Illinois, Indiana, Kansas, Kentucky, Maine, Massachusetts, Michigan, Missouri, Nebraska, New Hampshire, North Carolina, Ohio, Oklahoma, Pennsylvania, West Virginia); discard any recalled Taylor Farms de Mexico iceberg lettuce and do not eat shredded iceberg lettuce from affected Taco Bell locations, though the chain says it has already replaced the supply. If you shop at Walmart, check for recalled Marketside iceberg lettuce products. North Carolina is a special case: FDA and CDC now count it in the cluster, but NCDHHS's own health department still says its cases do not appear associated with the Taco Bell outbreak, so residents there are getting two different messages from federal and state authorities while that is reconciled. There is no need for panic and no basis for rumor-driven food bans.

For food-sector buyers and operators: the actionable lesson is supplier concentration. A single processor's iceberg lettuce reaching restaurants and grocery shelves in at least 31 states is the same failure geometry as 2013, when Taylor Farms de Mexico salad mix sickened 631 people across 25 states. If you cannot answer "which processor, which growing region, which lot" for your leafy greens within an hour, that is the gap this outbreak is exposing, and it is a gap you own regardless of what FDA concludes, or what Mexico's government disputes.

For leaders in food, healthcare, and public-health-adjacent sectors: treat this as a leading indicator. The durable story is not the parasite; it is whether the systems that find and stop foodborne outbreaks are keeping pace, and whether their conclusions hold up once other authorities weigh in. Those systems published a recall wider than the named cluster, reported a direct lab confirmation of contamination, retracted that confirmation as a false positive a day later, resolved a nine-day reporting freeze with a large, coherent catch-up update on Jul 22, and now face a source country's government publicly disputing the attribution while a second, unexplained outbreak runs concurrently, a genuinely mixed track record. Watch whether North Carolina's vehicle is identified, whether Mexico's dispute is resolved or escalates, whether the confirmed count keeps pace with state totals going forward, and federal surveillance funding, as the real signals.
